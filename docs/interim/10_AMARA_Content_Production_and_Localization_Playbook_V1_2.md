1. Purpose
This playbook defines how AMARA creates and rewrites public-facing copy across English, German,
Spanish, Dutch, and Swedish.
Its purpose is to solve a recurring content problem: a page must be useful to guests, searchable,
differentiated, factually credible, conversion-aware, and natural in the target language at the same time.
Trying to solve all of those requirements sentence by sentence usually produces artificial copy.
AMARA therefore separates the work into stages. Strategy defines what the page must achieve. Facts define
what may be claimed. Search research defines what people are looking for. Native-language writing defines
how a human would naturally say it. SEO and editorial review come after the first draft.
The core principle is:
     SEO determines which questions and concepts AMARA must cover. Native-language editorial
     judgment determines how a person would naturally express them. Verified AMARA facts
     determine what AMARA is allowed to claim.
This playbook does not change page types, routing, SEO runtime ownership, slugs, or structured-data
architecture.

2. What this playbook prevents
AMARA public copy must not drift into any of the following patterns:
-     keyword-first writing that sounds like SEO copy;
-     literal translation of an English, German, or other language master text;
-     internal strategy language appearing in guest-facing copy;
-     generic premium vocabulary used instead of evidence;
-     repeated claims about calm, quality, authenticity, curation, or design without concrete support;
-     invented sensory details or guest behavior;
-     long pages created to satisfy a word-count target rather than a real information need;
-     page-type contamination, such as turning an Explore page into an arrival manual or a Trust page into a
      destination essay;
-     copy that could be moved unchanged to hundreds of unrelated hospitality websites.
The target is not to make AI authorship undetectable. The target is writing that is useful, specific, original,
credible, and natural enough to meet the standard of a competent native editor.


3. The AMARA content team model
AMARA may use one person or one AI system to perform several roles, but the roles must remain
conceptually separate.

3.1 Content strategist
Defines:
-   page job;
-   page type;
-   guest decision or desired outcome;
-   information hierarchy;
-   conversion goal;
-   what the page must not become.

3.2 SEO editor
Defines:
-   search intent;
-   primary query family;
-   supporting topics, entities, and modifiers;
-   language-specific search vocabulary;
-   SERP differentiation opportunities;
-   any search question that the page must answer explicitly.
The SEO editor does not write the final sentences.

3.3 Product / proof editor
Builds the evidence inventory from current approved sources:
-   property facts and amenities;
-   location and access facts;
-   verified review evidence;
-   owner/host experience that is genuinely known;
-   approved official or editorial sources for destination facts;
-   approved live operational data where relevant.
This role prevents attractive but unsupported claims from entering the copy.

3.4 Native copywriter
Writes the page as a native editorial edition for the target language.
The copywriter receives the same facts and strategic intent as the other language editions, but does not
receive a sentence-level translation template that must be mirrored.

3.5 Human-voice / conversion editor
Removes:
-   translated phrasing;
-   AI patterns;
-   strategy vocabulary;
-   repetition;
-   unnecessary adjectives;
-   abstract claims that can be replaced by proof;
-   awkward CTAs;


-   content that is technically correct but emotionally distancing.
The editor also checks whether the page gives the guest a plausible reason to continue, compare, trust, or
book.

4. When a Page Brief is required
A full Page Brief is required for new public pages, material rewrites, new strategic page jobs, significant
section/story changes, and multi-language work where the content proposition is being created or
reconsidered.
A new full brief is not required for Class 0/1 work when all of the following remain stable:
-   page type and page job;
-   section structure and conversion role;
-   approved factual basis;
-   route/slug and runtime architecture;
-   the change is limited to existing content fields or equivalent localized copy.
For those cases, use the Content Fast Lane in Section 5. Reuse an existing approved brief instead of
recreating it.
When a Page Brief is required, drafting starts only after the brief is aligned.

4.1 Page identity
Record:
-   page name;
-   canonical page type (A/B/C/D);
-   parent/child role;
-   primary audience;
-   primary destination/property context;
-   current URL/slug status;
-   conversion path.

4.2 Page job
Write one sentence that answers:
    What should a guest understand, feel, compare, or decide after using this page?
The page job must be guest-facing. Avoid internal phrases such as "decision framework", "travel fit",
"conversion fork", "rhythm", or "authority layer" unless the guest would naturally use that language.

4.3 Search intent
Record:
-   primary search intent;
-   primary query family;
-   important secondary queries;
-   entities and modifiers;
-   likely comparison questions;
-   locale-specific wording differences.
Search intent is a coverage requirement, not a sentence template.

4.4 Guest needs and questions
Translate the search intent into normal human questions.


For example, a page about romantic stays may need to answer questions such as:
-   Which apartments are really best for two people?
-   Which have private terraces?
-   Which have the largest beds?
-   Which have double showers or outdoor showers?
-   Is the location quiet at night?
-   Can we walk to restaurants?
-   Which stay is better for a short trip and which for a longer stay?
These questions are more useful to a copywriter than the abstract instruction "position AMARA as a romantic
destination brand".

4.5 AMARA proof inventory
For each major promise, list the evidence that makes it credible.
Examples:
-   "good for couples" -> occupancy, bed size, private outdoor space, bathroom/shower layout, guest
    reviews, location context;
-   "easy beach stay" -> measured distance, lift/access, balcony, kitchen, nearby services;
-   "historic character" -> documented building history, restoration period, visible original fabric, approved
    local heritage facts;
-   "quiet" -> actual location characteristics or review evidence, not a generic adjective;
-   "personally hosted" -> real operating model and direct host contact.
If the proof inventory is weak, weaken or remove the claim. Do not compensate with stronger adjectives.

4.6 Constraints and negative facts
List relevant limitations that a guest should know, for example:
-   stairs;
-   no terrace;
-   no full kitchen;
-   smaller bed;
-   parking reality;
-   seasonal wind;
-   limited access;
-   distance from the beach;
-   no daily housekeeping.
Honesty is part of the product. However, limitations should be presented in the right order: first establish why
the option may be attractive, then explain the relevant constraint clearly and without drama.

4.7 Story and section order
Define the narrative before writing paragraphs.
A typical sequence is:
1. clear promise or answer;
2. concrete proof;
3. useful comparison or context;
4. practical limitation/caveat;
5. next useful action.


Not every page needs this exact order. The sequence must fit the page type.

4.8 Conversion goal
Define one primary next action and, where necessary, one secondary action.
Examples:
-   compare apartments;
-   check availability;
-   read verified reviews;
-   open a practical guide;
-   choose between Frigiliana and Nerja.
Do not add a CTA simply because the layout has space for one.

4.9 Length hypothesis
Estimate the amount of content needed to answer the page job completely.
This is a starting hypothesis, not a quota. The final page should be as long as necessary and as short as
possible.

5. Content Fast Lane
The Content Fast Lane exists to keep routine editorial work fast without lowering factual or language quality.
The operating principle is:
    Do not make a content edit more technical than it is.

5.1 When to use it
Use the Fast Lane when:
-   the page already exists and its job is clear;
-   the requested change is primarily copy in existing fields;
-   no slug, resolver, shared component, design system, or page architecture change is intended;
-   the factual claims are already established or can be checked directly;
-   the locale scope is clear.
Examples include:
-   replacing a German hero lead;
-   rewriting apartment-card descriptions;
-   improving a meta title/description without changing URL strategy;
-   changing FAQ wording;
-   making an existing paragraph more natural in one language;
-   updating several related content fields across a small batch of pages.

5.2 Fast Lane workflow
The default workflow is:
1. identify the current content owner and confirm the page type is unchanged;
2. verify only the facts affected by the new wording;
3. draft and approve the target-language copy;
4. implement the approved copy in the existing fields;
5. run targeted diff/source checks and inspect the rendered page when text length or placement could matter;


6. confirm other locales were not unintentionally changed;
7. commit locally when requested;
8. do not push unless an intentional deployment/release point has been agreed.
Do not require a second full content analysis after the copy has already been approved and its facts verified.

5.3 Escalation from content to structure
Stop the Fast Lane and classify the work as a local page change when the approved content genuinely
needs:
-   a new section or proof block;
-   section reordering;
-   new page-local markup;
-   a new local interaction or presentation pattern.
This escalation should remain narrow. A local markup need does not justify changing shared components,
global CSS, runtime infrastructure, or slugs unless a real blocker is proven.

5.4 Editorial and implementation roles
Separate roles by job, not by vendor.
A practical default is:
-   Editorial role - page job, facts, search intent, native copy, Human Voice, final copy approval.
-   Implementation role - repository ownership, field replacement, minimal local markup where approved,
    validation, and diff/status reporting.
For example, ChatGPT may act as the editorial role and Codex as the implementation role. This is an
operational convenience, not an architectural dependency.
Do not make both roles independently repeat the same research or copy strategy unless a contradiction or
new risk appears.

5.5 Efficient validation and batching
For string-only or field-only edits, use targeted checks while working.
Several validated Class 0/1 changes may be grouped locally before a full production build or release
validation, provided no structural/runtime change increases the risk in between.
Do not run the same full build, broad route matrix, or browser audit after every isolated text field solely out of
habit.
Before release, run the combined validation appropriate to the batch and current repository contracts.

5.6 Fast Lane completion test
A Fast Lane edit is complete when:
-   the approved copy is present in the intended field(s);
-   changed facts are verified;
-   the target language reads naturally;
-   page type and structure remain unchanged;
-   the affected rendered surface is plausible where visual review is relevant;
-   unaffected locales and unrelated files remain untouched;
-   the change is locally committed if requested;
-   no deployment/push has occurred unless explicitly approved.


6. Proof-before-Claim Rule
AMARA differentiates itself through real attributes, not through premium adjectives.
Prefer:
    "100 metres to Torrecilla beach, 500 metres to the Balcon de Europa, and a 200 x 200 cm bed."
instead of:
    "A premium coastal retreat for unforgettable romantic moments."
Prefer:
    "The private terrace gets afternoon sun; mornings are shaded and cooler for breakfast."
instead of:
    "A thoughtfully curated outdoor experience."
A benefit may be named, but the copy should show why the benefit is credible.

6.1 Claim test
For every material claim, ask:
1. What exactly are we claiming?
2. What is the evidence?
3. Is the evidence current?
4. Is the claim true for every property/page element that appears to be covered?
5. Can we state the proof directly instead of using an adjective?

6.2 No invented atmosphere
Do not invent:
-   sounds;
-   smells;
-   views;
-   guest routines;
-   owner habits;
-   weather effects;
-   local behavior;
-   "most guests do..." statements;
-   emotional reactions;
unless AMARA actually knows them and the statement is appropriate to publish.
Specificity is valuable only when it is true.

7. Native-Language Localization Rule
AMARA has five editorial editions: EN, DE, ES, NL, and SV.
The cross-language SSOT consists of:
-   facts;
-   page job;
-   claim boundaries;
-   search intent;
-   conversion intent;


-   required topics.
The cross-language SSOT does not consist of sentence structure.
The rule is:
    Facts and intended meaning are shared. Sentences are not.
Each locale may independently choose:
-   sentence length;
-   word order;
-   idiom;
-   examples;
-   headline phrasing;
-   level of directness;
-   transitions;
-   where a factual caveat is best placed;
-   which natural keyword variant is used.
No locale should sound like a translation of another locale.

7.1 German guest voice
For normal hospitality and destination copy, German uses ihr/euch consistently unless a page has an
explicitly approved formal context such as legal information or a professional property-sale process.

7.2 Translation warning signs
Review any sentence that contains:
-   a noun combination that sounds imported from English;
-   an adjective that a native speaker would rarely use in hospitality;
-   an abstract metaphor that exists only because the source language used one;
-   identical sentence structure across all five locales;
-   an English marketing term that has a natural local equivalent;
-   an unnatural direct translation of "curated", "thoughtful", "fit", "rhythm", "belonging", "retreat",
    "experience", or similar brand vocabulary.
These words are not universally forbidden. They are warning signals that require native-language judgment.

8. Human Voice Rule
AMARA should sound like a knowledgeable, warm, self-confident host who knows the properties and
destinations well.
The voice is:
-   concrete;
-   warm without sentimentality;
-   clear without sounding bureaucratic;
-   honest without leading with negatives;
-   confident without self-praise;
-   descriptive without over-writing;
-   useful without sounding like a consultant.

8.1 The host test
Ask:
    Would a good host actually say or write this sentence to a guest?


If not, rewrite it.

8.2 The swap test
Ask:
    Could this sentence appear unchanged on 1,000 unrelated boutique-hotel or holiday-rental
    websites?
If yes, replace generic branding with AMARA-specific evidence, observation, or detail.

8.3 The read-aloud test
Read the paragraph aloud.
Rewrite when:
-   the sentence is difficult to say naturally;
-   several abstract nouns appear in succession;
-   the same idea appears two or three times with different adjectives;
-   the paragraph contains too many em dashes, slogan fragments, or symmetrical triads;
-   the wording sounds more impressive than informative.

8.4 Common AI/brand patterns to challenge
These constructions require special scrutiny:
-   "not just X, but Y";
-   "where X meets Y";
-   "designed for slow mornings";
-   "space to breathe";
-   "a slower rhythm";
-   "the perfect balance";
-   "thoughtfully curated";
-   "unforgettable moments";
-   "authentic experience";
-   "every detail has been considered";
-   repeated lists of three emotional benefits;
-   strategy terms such as fit, decision, base, journey, rhythm, or framework used as guest copy.
They may occasionally be natural, but they must never become AMARA's default voice.

9. SEO Integration Rule
SEO is mandatory, but it is applied in two different moments.

9.1 Before drafting: coverage
The SEO brief defines:
-   what people search for;
-   what questions must be answered;
-   what entities and modifiers matter;
-   which location/property terms should appear naturally;
-   what differentiates the page from competing results.

9.2 After drafting: language-safe optimization
After the native draft exists, review:


-   title;
-   meta description;
-   H1;
-   section headings;
-   primary-topic clarity;
-   important semantic terms;
-   internal link labels;
-   missing search questions;
-   unnecessary keyword repetition.
Do not damage native language to preserve an exact-match phrase.

9.3 Search snippet rule
A search title/description should answer two questions quickly:
1. What is this page about?
2. Why might this AMARA result be worth clicking instead of another result?
Generic hygiene statements such as "clear information about location and amenities" are not differentiation.
Prefer actual differences: historic apartments, private terraces, measured beach distance, specific location,
verified reviews, direct host operation, or another true page-specific reason.

10. Content Length & Density Rule
AMARA does not use a universal SEO word-count target.
The standard is:
    Complete enough to satisfy the page job; short enough that repetition and filler have been
    removed.

10.1 Type A - Location Authority
May be longer when the guest needs practical context, comparisons, caveats, and local orientation. Authority
comes from completeness and specificity, not from volume alone.

10.2 Type B - Explore
Should create desire and help discovery while providing enough practical context to choose what to explore
next. Do not turn the page into a logistics manual.

10.3 Type C - Trust
Usually benefits from restraint. Lead with evidence, responsibility, reviews, process, or real host perspective
rather than long philosophy sections.

10.4 Type D - Conversion
Prioritize the offer, differentiators, fit, constraints, proof, and next action. Do not bury the property/product
under destination prose.

10.5 Utility/Internal
Instruction clarity overrides brand voice. Say exactly what the guest needs to do.

11. Productizing Emotional Promises
AMARA should translate abstract guest desires into concrete product evidence.


For example, "romantic stay" is not enough as a content proposition. The page must explain what makes a
stay work well for two people.
Possible proof categories include, where factually applicable:
-   occupancy designed for two;
-   bed dimensions;
-   private terraces;
-   double showers;
-   outdoor showers;
-   hammocks;
-   privacy;
-   kitchen or outdoor kitchen;
-   walkability to restaurants;
-   sunset or sea view where verified;
-   quiet nighttime location where supported;
-   short-stay suitability;
-   verified couple reviews.
The editorial task is to connect the guest desire to the real product:
    guest desire -> concrete AMARA feature -> guest benefit -> proof
This is stronger than repeating a label such as "romantic", "premium", or "boutique".

12. Page-Type Integrity in Copy
The AMARA Core remains binding.

Type A - Location Authority
Write as a knowledgeable destination host. Help the guest understand the place and the practical
consequences of staying there. Do not turn the page into a sales essay.

Type B - Explore
Write for curiosity and selection. Help the guest discover beaches, food, hiking, events, or other experiences.
Do not overload with accommodation logistics.

Type C - Trust
Write to reduce uncertainty and establish credibility. Use evidence and direct language. Do not become an
emotional destination guide.

Type D - Conversion
Write around the actual property or conversion offer. Show concrete differences and constraints. Do not
substitute generic brand language for product information.


12.1 Type A - Location Decision Pattern
For Type A location content, destination information should be translated into consequences for an overnight
stay rather than presented as isolated tourism facts.

A useful editorial pattern is:

   Place -> Overnight consequence -> AMARA fit or limitation -> Verifiable proof -> Guest decision ->
   Natural next step

Place
State the relevant physical, logistical, climatic, or structural reality of the destination.

Overnight consequence
Explain what that reality changes when a guest stays for several consecutive nights.

AMARA fit or limitation
Connect AMARA only when a real property, micro-location, or operating fact changes the consequence. If
AMARA does not solve the issue, say so. If another AMARA location better fits the guest's priority, that may
be the correct recommendation.

Verifiable proof
Support material AMARA claims with canonical property facts, first-party measurement, verified guest
evidence, or appropriate authoritative external sources. The physical origin of a fact and the system that
stores it are separate concerns: once verified, the approved AMARA data/content layer remains the
publishing authority.

Guest decision
Help the reader understand what the information means for the choice of location or stay model.

Natural next step
Route to a relevant Authority, Explore, Trust, or Conversion surface only after the decision context makes that
step useful.

Type A authority must not turn every location fact into a product benefit. Honest limitations and alternative
recommendations increase the credibility of genuine advantages.

First-party location knowledge should be specific enough to add information that a generic destination guide
cannot provide, but it must not invent precision that has not actually been measured or verified.

13. Editorial Workflow
This full workflow applies to material new copy and rewrites that require a Page Brief under Section 4. Class
0/1 edits use the Fast Lane in Section 5 unless they escalate.

Step 1 - Build the brief
Do not draft yet.


Step 2 - Verify proof
Mark each material fact as:
-   confirmed;
-   needs verification;
-   time-sensitive;
-   property-specific;
-   location-specific.
Remove or defer unsupported claims.

Step 3 - Build the story
Draft headings and section purpose only. Confirm that each section has one job.

Step 4 - Write the native draft
Write naturally without trying to place every keyword.

Step 5 - Run the SEO pass
Add missing concepts and improve search clarity without making the prose unnatural.

Step 6 - Run the human-voice pass
Use the host test, swap test, read-aloud test, and AI-pattern review.

Step 7 - Run the plausibility / conversion pass
Ask:
-   Is the promise believable?
-   Does the proof make the differentiation clear?
-   Are limitations framed honestly and proportionally?
-   Does the CTA follow naturally from the content?
-   Would a guest know what makes this page/property different?

Step 8 - Validate in the real page
Review the rendered/runtime page, not only the content object.
Check:
-   visual text density;
-   heading balance;
-   line length;
-   repeated wording across cards/sections;
-   CTA frequency;
-   whether the page still feels natural when scanned rather than read linearly.

15. Final Editorial Checklist
A material public page is ready when all of the following are true:
-   The page job is clear in one sentence.
-   The page remains pure to its AMARA page type.
-   Search intent and native keyword family were defined before drafting.
-   Important guest questions are answered.
-   Every material product or destination claim has a credible source.
-   The copy shows real differentiation instead of relying on premium adjectives.
-   The language sounds native rather than translated.
-   Internal strategy terminology is absent from guest-facing prose unless genuinely natural.
-   No sensory or behavioral detail has been invented.
-   Keywords appear naturally and are not repeated mechanically.
-   Title and meta description explain both topic and click-worthy differentiation.
-   Repetition and filler have been removed.
-   Limitations are honest but do not dominate the opening of the page.
-   CTA wording and placement follow the page job.
-   The text has been reviewed in the actual rendered page.
-   Locale-scoped work has not unintentionally changed other languages.

16. Working principle
AMARA does not need to sound more luxurious than it is. It needs to make the real value easy to see.
The strongest copy usually comes from details that already exist: a bed size, a private terrace, a restoration
story, a measured distance, a real constraint, a verified review, a route guests actually walk, or an
observation that only someone who knows the place could make.


The operator should be able to describe the desired content result without needing to know whether the
implementation lives in an Astro page, a content object, or another internal owner. The system must translate
that intent into the smallest safe edit.
The goal is not more copy or more process. The goal is more truth, better selection, more natural
language, and the smallest workflow that can deliver those outcomes safely.
